Research project · Syzygy Planetary Manifold Telemetry

A weather forecast
for computer networks

Working prototype

Big computer networks — the kind that move data around the world — are hard to watch. By the time a slowdown shows up on a dashboard, people are already feeling it. Syzygy keeps two pictures of the network at once: one of what's happening right now, and one of what's likely to happen half a second from now. When the two stop matching, that's an early warning — congestion building, hardware overheating, traffic heading the wrong way — while there's still time to fix it.

Two pictures, compared live

One view tracks the network exactly as it is. The other predicts the next moment — delays, bottlenecks, and slowdowns on their way. Syzygy compares them constantly and raises a flag the moment reality and prediction drift apart.

Serious math, shown simply

Under the hood, Syzygy uses advanced mathematics to model how delays and load ripple across a network — that math is the product, not decoration. On screen, you simply see a clear map of the network and where trouble is forming.

Every change has to explain itself

Syzygy keeps a receipt for every change it records: what happened, what caused it, and how the numbers add up. If the explanation doesn't balance, the reading isn't trusted. Nothing is a black box.

Summaries in plain words

An AI writer turns the raw numbers into a short story a person can read, while the map lets you zoom from the whole network down to a single problem spot.

Research project · AI that runs on the device itself

Quadra Kestrel

Research system

Most AI lives in a data center and needs the internet to think. Quadra Kestrel is research into AI that runs on the device itself — a drone, a sensor, a small robot — where there's no connection and decisions have to happen in a split second. It's powered by our Quadra-Matrix system, which borrows ideas from how real brains learn: it practices, adjusts, and keeps getting better as conditions change.

Learns like a brain, runs on a chip

Kestrel's AI is modeled on real brain cells, so it learns from experience instead of following one fixed script — and it can also follow plain written rules, so its decisions stay sensible.

A complete workshop, not just a model

Training tools, live progress dashboards, and step-by-step guides for testing what the AI has learned — and where it still falls short.

Tested hard before it flies

Every stage has its own tests and written summaries. The point is to find the AI's mistakes in the lab — not in the air.

Fits wherever it's needed

Packaged with standard tools (Docker and Kubernetes) so the same system can run on one work computer, on a small device at the edge, or across a whole fleet.

First test case — self-flying drones: a drone can't phone a data center in the middle of a flight. Kestrel is being shaped to sit inside the flight controller itself, so the drone can react instantly — even with no connection at all.
